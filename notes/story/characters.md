# Characters — Who Killed Uskar?

This document is the canonical character bible for the game. All character names, spellings, and details here supersede earlier notes. The canonical spelling of the murdered individual is **USKAR** (not Uskerr — that was an early inconsistency in older notes).

---

## Uskar

The murdered individual. The game's central mystery and absent center.

### Identity and Role

Uskar's identity, role, and importance are deliberately unknown at the start of the game. Uncovering who Uskar was — and why they died — is the game's core investigative thread. What is established: Uskar possessed a soul of extraordinary significance. Their death was consequential enough to set an entire chain of metaphysical events in motion and draw the direct attention of an Outer God.

Uskar may have been a god, a legendary mage, or a creature of rare purity — the ambiguity is intentional and should be preserved until the game earns the revelation.

### Thematic Significance

Uskar is the absent center. Every character, room, clue, and motivation in the game orbits them. They never appear in person — they are dead when the game begins — yet their presence is felt in every discovery the player makes. Uskar functions as the hole in the world that the story keeps falling into.

## The Original Vessel

The player's first controlled character. Their name and origin are deliberately withheld at the start.

### Identity and Background

A creature of ambiguous background found in Room 1 with no memory of how they arrived or why they are there. Whether their presence is coincidence or orchestration is itself a question. They may be connected to Uskar. They may have been placed in Room 1 intentionally by Silarmousch as a pawn.

### Fate

They die in Room 4 by drinking Bol's poison nectar. Their death is the transition point at which the Outer God (the player) passes into Bol as the new vessel.

### Gameplay Role

The player's first vessel. Controlled through Rooms 1 to 4. Their death ends the first phase of the game.

### Thematic Significance

The original vessel represents the disposable instrument — a creature used, moved, sinned through, and discarded. Their unresolved identity invites the question of whether any vessel is truly known or simply a vehicle for forces larger than themselves.

## Lisspeg

A simple, earnest creature who waited faithfully in Room 1.

### Personality

Devout, naive, genuinely good-hearted. Lisspeg believes deeply in prophecy and divine purpose. He is not sophisticated. He is not strategic. He is simply faithful and full of love for whatever higher purpose he has been entrusted with.

### Role

Silarmousch gave Lisspeg a key and told him that a "promised prophet" would come. Lisspeg waited. When the player (original vessel) arrives, Lisspeg is overcome with joy. He calls them the prophet. He hands over the key willingly, without hesitation or suspicion.

### Fate

Killed by the player (original vessel) in Room 1 with the Sinner's Dagger. This is the game's first cardinal sin. Lisspeg does not resist. He does not fight back. He dies with his trust intact and his faith unbroken.

### Future Possibility

Later in the game, the player — now as an Outer God — may return through time, possess Lisspeg's soul, and play as him. This would reveal Lisspeg's unique perspective on events the player already witnessed, and open paths that were closed to the original vessel.

### Thematic Significance

Lisspeg represents innocence instrumentalized by manipulative forces. He is good, and his goodness is the very thing that makes him useful as a tool. His death is the game's moral inciting incident — the moment the player is made complicit in something they cannot undo.

## Silarmousch

A powerful mage. The architect of the demonic trap in Rooms 1 through 3.

### Personality

Cold, calculating, masterful. Silarmousch does not act directly when indirect manipulation will suffice. He designs systems — social, metaphysical, spatial — and lets others walk into them. He is not impulsive. Every piece he places has a purpose.

### Actions

- Placed Lisspeg in Room 1, gave him a key, and told him a prophet would come.
- Planted the Sinner's Dagger in Room 2.
- Designed Room 3's demonic path.

### Motives

Deliberately ambiguous. Possible theories, none confirmed:

1. He wants the Outer God corrupted to serve demonic ends.
2. He is trying to free creatures from divine control by causing gods to destroy their own souls through the manipulation of other souls.
3. He has his own private agenda connected to Uskar's death — one that neither the player nor Bournache yet understands.

A proposed elaboration of theory 3 is recorded in [narrative.md](narrative.md) ("Silarmousch's Escape Plan"): Uskar caused Silarmousch's creation, so Silarmousch's end goal is to steer the Outer God into travelling back and killing Uskar — forking the timeline into a branch where Silarmousch was never created and is therefore out of the god's reach.

### Current Status

Unknown. Likely still active in the world, watching from a distance.

### Thematic Significance

Silarmousch represents the misuse of knowledge — the cold application of intelligence toward opaque ends. He understands systems deeply enough to corrupt them without appearing to touch them. He is the manipulator who is never in the room when the harm happens.

## Bournache

A divine mage. The counter-force to Silarmousch's trap.

### Personality

Wise, selfless, principled. Bournache does not value his own life above what he believes is right. He is not reckless — his sacrifice is deliberate and considered. He acts out of conviction, not impulse.

### Actions

- Altered Room 3 via a magical painting, redirecting its nature.
- Left an unmissable note to warn the player.
- Appears in Room 3 to offer his sacrifice in person.
- Prior to his own death, arranged for Bol to be positioned in Room 4 with the poison nectar.

### Sacrifice

Bournache gives his entire soul to the player's vessel, purifying it — and dies. He chose this.

### Status

Dead by the time the player reaches Room 4.

### Thematic Significance

Bournache represents divine grace and willing sacrifice. His death mirrors the game's broader theme of purification through loss. Where Silarmousch builds traps, Bournache builds exits. His sacrifice is the answer to the question Silarmousch's trap asks.

## Bol

A young apprentice mage. The player's second vessel, from Room 4 onward.

### Personality

Earnest, curious, slightly anxious. Bol is not naive — he has a mage's education and genuine talent — but he is young enough that the weight of extraordinary events still shows on him. He is genuinely fond of his teachers.

### Background

Trained first by Bournache; now mentored by Sekmidorgue. He is serious about his craft and his relationships. He did not choose to be part of any of this.

### Role

Placed in Room 4 by Bournache's prior arrangement, carrying the poison nectar. He does not know the nectar will kill the original vessel — or he understands that it will cause them to pass beyond, which he interprets as "making the door visible" in a spiritual sense. Either reading holds. He gives the nectar without deception as he understands it.

### Effect of Possession

When the Outer God takes Bol, the possession is total: Bol loses all will and consciousness for as long as he is held, and he is not released until the vessel dies. The strength and purity of his soul make him an excellent vessel — a clean channel with full access to magic.

> **Open conflict — needs a decision.** This section, and the Thematic Significance below, were written under the older model in which a possessed creature stayed conscious and was gradually changed by the god. The revised model in [world/gods.md](../world/gods.md) makes possession total (no consciousness, no resistance, no release until death). Bol's arc as "ignorant pawn → awakened vessel who is sharpened and broken over time" no longer fits cleanly: a creature with no consciousness during possession cannot experience or visibly drift. Decide how Bol's arc and the free-will theme work under total possession before designing his Act 3–4 scenes.

### Thematic Significance

Bol represents the transition from ignorant pawn to awakened vessel. He did not ask to carry a god, but he carries one. His arc traces how divine possession transforms a creature — sharpening and breaking simultaneously, the gift inseparable from the cost.

## Sekmidorgue

A high mage at the Altar of Mages.

### Personality

Stern, formal, but genuinely invested in Bol's development. Sekmidorgue's discipline is not coldness — it is the form his care takes. He has standards because he respects his students enough to hold them to something.

### Role

Primary teacher and guide during the tutorial phase of Rooms 5 through 10. He knows that Bol is now carrying an Outer God. He treats this as extraordinary and sacred — not alarming, not threatening, but requiring careful navigation.

### Status

Alive and present as of Room 5.

## Historical Figures

In-world figures referenced in myth and NPC dialogue. They are mythological or historical — not present in the game's current timeline — but their stories shape how characters understand divine possession, sacrifice, and the demonic path.

### Gounargoun

A creature who fully surrendered to divine possession and achieved great deeds. Their story ends in nirvana — complete dissolution into the divine. A mythological figure invoked as the ideal of what total surrender to possession can become.

### Pyutkah

A creature who sought to meet a god through pilgrimages across their entire life. They never managed it through effort or intention. Finally, through self-sacrifice, the connection was achieved. Pyutkah's story is a parable about the limits of seeking and the necessity of release.

### Zortaug

A creature who chose the demonic path and became a monster. Invoked as a cautionary figure. The counterpoint to Gounargoun — the same crossroads, the opposite choice, the opposite end.

### Daghal

A trickster and deceiver who mastered morphing and used others' sacrifices for personal gain. Unlike Zortaug, who simply chose evil, Daghal exploited the system — taking what others gave up without paying the cost themselves. Current status unknown. The unresolved ending is deliberate: some cautionary figures don't have clean endings.

### Sinali

A great painter who became a mage and discovered magic paintings. The likely origin of the tradition Bournache draws on when he alters Room 3. Sinali's discovery established that art could be a vector for magical intervention — that a painting is not just representation but presence.
