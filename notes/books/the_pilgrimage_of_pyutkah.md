# The Pilgrimage of Pyutkah
### Being a True Account of His Wanderings, His Failures, and His Final Hour

---

*Transcribed from the original manuscript held in the Archive of the Unburdened. The compiler of this edition has made every effort to preserve the author's voice, including his occasional asides, his inconsistencies of chronology, and the long silences between entries that speak as clearly as any written word. This is not a book about triumph. Readers seeking triumphant stories are directed to the shelf behind you.*

*— Compiled by the Archivist of the Seventh Gate, in the year of the Sealed Season*

---

## A Note on Pyutkah

He was, by most accounts, an unremarkable creature before his forty-third year of life. He traded in dried cloth and lamp oil across the three southern districts, kept careful records, ate simple meals, and was remembered by his neighbors as a creature of measured temperament who asked no particular questions about the world.

Then, one evening in his forty-third year, he set his ledger down mid-entry, sat very still for a long time, and in the morning told his apprentice to manage the accounts indefinitely. He never returned to the trade.

What happened that evening, he described only once, in a letter to a fellow pilgrim he met during his third wandering:

*"I was writing a number. A small number — a difference in inventory, something trivial. And I thought: I will write this number and then another number and then another number until I die. And I thought: before I die, is there nothing else I am supposed to touch? I had heard of Gounargoun. Everyone has heard of Gounargoun. I could not sleep. I left in the morning."*

He was, at the time of his departure, not a religious creature in any formal sense. He did not belong to a temple order, had not completed a purification rite, and knew the doctrine of souls only as a creature knows anything heard in passing — partially, with large gaps filled by assumption. What he had was not theology. It was longing.

This is the record of what that longing cost him, and what it finally gave.

## Part One: The Temple of the First Gate

*From the personal journal of Pyutkah, first pilgrimage*

I arrived at the Temple of the First Gate after forty-two days of walking. I had not counted the days while walking; I counted them only when I arrived and looked back. The road had been long but not unkind. I was welcomed at wayhouses and given food without much asking. I think I looked sincere enough that creatures trusted I would repay it someday, which was probably true.

The temple is larger than I had imagined. The inscriptions on the outer walls are in a script I cannot read, but I was told by the keeper of the gate that they are not prayers — they are records of what has been witnessed here. Every time a divine presence was perceived within these walls, a mark was added. The walls are covered.

This moved me greatly, the first day. I sat in the great hall and felt certain that something immense had moved through this place many times and would move through it again, and that perhaps my waiting here was a form of answering a call I did not yet understand.

I stayed through the summer. I rose before light each day and performed the purification rites as the temple keepers had taught me — the washing of the hands, the recitation of the fourteen attributes of the soul, the period of stillness that follows. I did not eat meat. I did not speak carelessly. I kept careful account of my thoughts, and when a prideful or uncharitable thought arose, I noted it and released it as best I could.

Autumn came. The other pilgrims who had arrived at the same time began to leave. Some had received what they came for — small signs, they said, feelings of warmth, moments of clarity during prayer that felt borrowed from somewhere outside themselves. I experienced none of this. I examined myself to determine if I had performed the rites incorrectly, and concluded that I had not.

I stayed through winter. The cold settled into the stone walls, and the temple offered braziers but not enough of them, and several nights I lay under my blanket and could not sleep and stared at the ceiling and thought about inventory.

Not about my trade — I did not want to return to my trade. I thought about the inventory of my life: what I had done, what I had not done, what I had given to others and what I had kept for myself. I tried to be honest. I think I was mostly honest. I concluded that I was not a wicked creature, and not an especially good one either, and that if a god were selecting among the creatures of the world based on merit alone I would place somewhere in the middle of a long list.

This did not comfort me.

Spring came. I had been at the temple for three full seasons, and nothing had occurred.

I prayed on my last morning there. I did not pray with any particular demand — I had learned by then that demanding things in prayer felt like shouting into a well and then being angry at the water for not answering. I prayed with what I believed was genuine openness. I was wrong, probably. I know that now. But I was doing my best.

I left the temple on a clear morning. The keeper of the gate said: "Come back when you are ready," and I did not know what he meant. I walked for two days before I cried, and then I cried for a long time on the side of a road, and a passing creature gave me a piece of bread, and I ate it, and continued.

## Part Two: The Mountain of the Divine Wound

*From the personal journal of Pyutkah, second pilgrimage*

The story of the Mountain of the Divine Wound is known to every creature who has spent any time at all in the religious houses of the northern territories. A creature was standing on its slope — a shepherd, by most accounts, though some say a mage — when divine fire descended and struck the earth nearby. The creature was not harmed. The ground was scorched. The grass never grew back in that pattern, and when you stand at the site, even now, you can see the shape of where the fire touched: a circle about the width of three creatures standing shoulder to shoulder, dark against the hill, with a clear boundary, as though the divine presence knew exactly how much space it needed and required no more.

I had hoped to feel something there.

I felt the cold coming off the mountain. I felt the roughness of the path under my feet. I stood inside the circle of the old scar and closed my eyes and tried to let whatever was there enter me. The wind moved my cloak. A bird called somewhere below.

I stayed three weeks. I met several other pilgrims, and in them I found something valuable: the relief of shared frustration. We lit fires together in the evenings and talked about what we had tried and what had not worked, and the talking was honest in the way that only shared failure makes possible.

One pilgrim — a creature who had been wandering for eleven years and who wore his difficulty openly, in the lines of his face and the careful way he held his cup, as though he was always afraid of spilling something — told me he had once felt, very briefly, a sensation during his fifth year that he believed was divine. It lasted perhaps the span of three heartbeats.

"Was it worth eleven years?" I asked him.

He thought about it. "I don't know. But I don't know what I would have done instead."

I understood this completely. I understood it in a way I could not have understood it a year earlier, before I had left the cloth trade and the ledgers and the small, numbered life.

I left the mountain no closer to the divine. But I was also not discouraged in the way I had been after leaving the Temple of the First Gate. Something in me was settling. A kind of patience I had not known I had access to was beginning to make itself available.

I did not yet understand that patience is not the same as readiness.

## Part Three: The Middle Years

*A summary of the third through seventh pilgrimages, compiled from letters and later recollections, as the journals of these years were lost in a fire at the wayhouse of Margouran*

Over the next twelve years, Pyutkah visited the following sacred sites: the Waterfall of the Second Naming, where a god is said to have spoken aloud in a language that could be heard by creatures for the first and only time; the Ruin of the House That Received Light, a structure struck by divine illumination during the ancient conflicts and never rebuilt; the Salt Flat of the Listening, which receives no rain and where it is said that sound travels differently — or, as some accounts have it, that the divine receives sound differently; and two other sites of lesser fame but equal significance in the traditions of the regional pilgrimage orders.

He was, by those who encountered him during these years, remembered variously as "deeply earnest," "a creature clearly not at peace," and "kind, in the way that people are kind when they want something and know that kindness is a form of worthiness."

In his letters from this period, a new tone enters the writing.

He writes to his niece in the sixth year of this period:

*"I am told that Gounargoun did not seek the divine. I am told it simply found him. He was doing something ordinary — something I have been told was not even particularly virtuous — when it happened. He did not fast beforehand. He had not completed a full cycle of purification rites. He was simply there, and whatever he was, it was enough. I am not angry. I am trying not to be angry. I am a creature who has spent more time in purification than Gounargoun spent breathing, probably, and I don't know what the difference between us is. I wish I knew what the difference between us was."*

He writes to a fellow pilgrim in the same period:

*"Do you ever think that we are doing something wrong? Not morally wrong — I mean structurally wrong. That there is a mistake in the mechanism of what we are doing. I don't know what we would do instead. But I think about it."*

It is during the sixth pilgrimage, at the Ruin of the House That Received Light, that Pyutkah encountered an old mage — unnamed in all surviving accounts, referred to only as "the old mage of Ruin-Stair" — who sat at the foot of a broken wall each morning and said almost nothing, and who, on the third day, said to Pyutkah:

*"You seek the god. The god already knows you. What the god waits for is not your arrival at a holy place. It waits for you to stop arriving."*

Pyutkah wrote in a later account that he considered this for a few moments, decided the old mage was being deliberately obscure in the way that mages sometimes are when they want to seem wise without committing to a specific wisdom, and walked away. He was not unkind about it. He simply could not use the words.

He carried them with him, however. He was too honest a creature not to carry them.

## Part Four: The Final Pilgrimage

*From the account given by a priest of the Temple of the Third Gate, who witnessed the events described. Recorded in the Archive of the Unburdened approximately four years after the events occurred.*

I should say first that I did not know Pyutkah personally before I encountered him on the road to the Temple of the Third Gate. I knew the name. Those of us who have spent time among the pilgrimage routes know it — not because he was famous, but because he had become a kind of story that pilgrims tell each other, not always charitably. He was the creature who never stopped. The creature who kept arriving at sacred places and finding nothing and keeping on. Some told it as an admirable story. Others told it as a cautionary one.

He looked older than I had expected. Not frail — he walked with no difficulty, and his eyes were sharp — but settled in the way that creatures get when they have been carrying a weight for many years and have made a kind of peace with its presence without ever setting it down.

I asked him, on our second day walking together, what he expected from this pilgrimage.

He considered my question for longer than I expected.

"Nothing," he said at last. "I expect nothing. I have been expecting things for twenty years and the expecting has not helped. This time I am going because I don't know what else to do with the years I have left, and walking toward something always felt more honest to me than sitting still."

I thought this was perhaps the truest thing I had heard a pilgrim say in many years of walking with pilgrims. I told him so.

He smiled in the careful way of a creature who is not sure the compliment is warranted. "I think I am just tired," he said.

We arrived at the Temple of the Third Gate on the morning of the fifth day. It is a smaller temple than the Temple of the First Gate — more intimate, set into a hillside, the approach paved with pale stones that catch the light in the morning. There is a divine door on the western side of the inner courtyard. I had seen it many times. It is a beautiful door: tall, framed in a material that is neither stone nor wood, that reads to the eye as something older than both.

Beside this door, on the morning of our arrival, stood a creature unlike any I had seen before.

I say this with full awareness of how strange it sounds. I have seen many creatures, of many kinds. This one was different in a way I cannot locate precisely. It was young — young in the way that suggests not merely a short life but a kind of freshness that has nothing to do with age, as though this creature had not been in this world long enough to be completely shaped by it. And it was standing before the divine door with the quality of attention that I associate with creatures who are not looking at a door but through it — who are so aware of what is on the other side that the object itself becomes almost transparent to them.

But it could not pass. The door did not open.

I understood the mechanics of this. The creature's soul was not pure enough to meet the door's requirement. I had seen this before, in my years at the temples — creatures who stood before divine thresholds they could not cross, some angry, some in despair, some in a kind of patient agony. What I had not seen before was what I saw in this particular creature: not frustration, but urgency. Not the urgency of wanting, but the urgency of needing. As though something depended on it that was larger than itself.

Pyutkah stopped beside me and looked at the creature for a long time.

I do not know what he saw. I only know what he said afterward.

"It is like reading about something for twenty years," he said to me quietly, "and then walking into the room where the thing actually is."

He walked toward the creature. I followed, at a distance.

## Part Five: The Last Conversation

*Continued from the same account*

The young creature looked at Pyutkah with those eyes that seemed to see too much, and Pyutkah looked back, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

Then the creature said, in a voice that was not exactly like ordinary speech — not louder or stranger, but somehow more present, as though each word occupied a little more space than words usually do:

"I need to pass through this door. My soul is not sufficient. I need a willing sacrifice."

I had read of this. The sacrificial rite is documented in the old texts: one creature willingly gives its soul to another, strengthening what was insufficient. The one who gives does not survive the giving. Every account I had read of this rite described it as a theoretical possibility, an extreme measure, something discussed in texts but not commonly performed in living memory.

Pyutkah was quiet for a long moment.

I expected him to ask questions. He was an honest creature — I had already learned that — and honest creatures ask questions before they commit to enormous things. I expected him to ask who the young creature was, or where it came from, or what lay on the other side of the door.

He did not ask any of these things.

He asked only: "Is it willing? The ritual — it must be willing, yes? No one can take it from me?"

"Yes," the creature said. "It must be freely given."

Pyutkah nodded. He looked at the door for a moment — looked at it the way you look at something you have been searching for when you finally understand that the thing you were searching for was never in the direction you were walking.

"Then take it," he said.

I moved forward then, because I am a priest and I know the rite, and Pyutkah needed someone to speak the words and hold the space. He did not seem surprised that I knew what to do. He positioned himself calmly, with the stillness of a creature who has spent twenty years learning how to be still, and I began the recitation.

I will not transcribe the ritual words here. They are held in the rite for a reason.

I will say only: he was calm throughout. Not the calm of someone who has suppressed fear, but a deeper calm, the kind that comes when a creature has moved past the point where fear would be useful. His eyes stayed open. He looked at the young creature. And as the rite progressed, something in his expression changed — not dramatically, not in any way I could name precisely — but as though some long pressure had been released.

His soul passed into the young creature. The mechanics of this are not visible to the eye, but the effects are immediate and unmistakable: the young creature stood taller, and the divine door — which had been sealed, had been sealed every morning I had seen it for many years — opened.

Pyutkah's body settled slowly onto the pale stones. Not collapsed — settled. Like something placed carefully.

I knelt beside him. His eyes were open. His face held an expression I have thought about every day since.

He was not at peace in the way a sleeping creature is at peace. He was at peace in the way that a creature is when it has finally arrived somewhere after a very long journey and recognized the place as the one it was always looking for.

He did not speak again.

I believe — and I hold this with the same uncertainty I hold all beliefs about divine matters — that in the moment of the giving, he felt what he had spent twenty years seeking. Not at a sacred site. Not after a season of purification. In the act itself: the one act in which he was not trying to receive anything at all.

The old mage of Ruin-Stair had been right. The god had been waiting for him to stop arriving.

He stopped arriving, finally, by giving himself completely away.

## Afterword

*On what was found at the Temple of the Third Gate*

The young creature passed through the divine door. Those of us who witnessed this have told the account differently in different years, as witnesses do — memories settle differently than events — but on this detail every account agrees: the door opened, the creature passed, and it did not return.

Where it went, I cannot say. Nor can I say what it carried with it, beyond the soul of a creature who spent twenty years seeking something and found it only by releasing the seeking entirely.

Pyutkah's body was interred in the grounds of the temple, as is the custom for creatures who die on sacred soil.

At the foot of the door-frame where he had stood, some days after the event, a flower appeared. Those who tend the temple grounds have noted that it does not follow the ordinary rhythms of seasons. It blossoms at irregular intervals, without observable pattern — except that the priests have noticed, over the years of keeping record, that it tends to open when someone passes through the door who carries something that was not originally their own.

The temple keeps no official position on what this signifies.

I think Pyutkah would have found it funny that the flower is noted but not explained. He spent twenty years looking for explanations. He found, in the end, something that needed no explaining, only doing.

That seems right for him.

*— Account concluded. Witness: Priest of the Third Gate, whose name is withheld by his own request*
