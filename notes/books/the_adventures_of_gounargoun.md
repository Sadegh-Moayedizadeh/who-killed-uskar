# The Adventures of Gounargoun
### Being the True Account of the Blessed Vessel, His Great Deeds, and His Final Passage Beyond the Reach of Gods and Creatures Alike

*Transcribed and annotated by an unnamed scholar of the Ninth Order, from fragments preserved in the temple archives of the High Sanctuary, in the reign of the god whose name may not be written*

## Foreword by the Transcriber

Reader, what you hold in your hands is among the oldest documents in our keeping — older, some say, than the temple itself, older perhaps than the language in which it was first set down. I have labored for the better part of two decades to render it into contemporary script without losing the quality of its original voice, which is unlike any other writing I have encountered in forty years of scholarship.

The original text does not name its author. It names no sources. It makes no claims of biography. It presents itself simply as *a telling* — as if the story required no author, only a throat through which to pass.

I have divided the text into the chapters which seemed most natural to me. Whether Gounargoun was one creature or a composite of many, whether the events described belong to a single life or a thousand lives collapsed into one, I will not venture to say. What I can say is this: the text has a coherence that resists the arguments of scholars who would call it allegory. Something happened. Someone became something no creature had been before.

What that means for the rest of us — for those of us who pray and receive no answer, or who receive an answer and are too frightened to open our hands and let it in — I leave to the reader.

The soul is a door. That much I have learned from this text, and perhaps it is all I needed to learn.

*— The Unnamed Transcriber*

## Part the First: The Craftsman of Vel Amourath

In the time before the great accountings, when the world was younger by half than it is now and the gods had not yet grown distant, there lived in the village of Vel Amourath a craftsman of modest reputation and extraordinary patience.

His name was Gounargoun, though in those days it was a common enough name, and no one who met him would have thought to write it down.

He was a maker of bowls. This should not be read as metaphor. He made bowls — fired clay, shaped by his hands into vessels for water and soup and the carrying of small useful things. He had been trained by his mother, who had been trained by her mother before her, and so on back through generations that stretched beyond the village's memory. The family had always made bowls. The village had always needed them. There was, in this arrangement, a kind of harmony so quiet it was indistinguishable from silence.

Gounargoun was not unhappy. He was not particularly happy, either. He was a craftsman who did his work well, who ate what he needed, who slept when his body required it, and who prayed each evening without expectation.

This last detail is important. He prayed every evening. Not because he believed prayer would change his circumstances — the gods had never answered him directly, and he was old enough to have given up the childish hope that they would. He prayed because the act of prayer itself had become the point. It was the only moment of his day that was not in service of something else. When he shaped a bowl, it was in service of the family who would use it. When he ate, he was in service of the body that needed fuel. But when he prayed, he was in service of nothing he could name. He simply turned toward whatever vast thing lay behind the world and said, in his own rough way: *I know you are there. I do not understand you. I am here.*

For thirty years he prayed this prayer, in nearly those exact words, and nothing happened.

And then one evening, at the end of the autumn when the light came down through the window of his workshop in a particular slant that turned the dust in the air to suspended gold, something happened.

He was mid-prayer. His eyes were open, as they always were — he had never found closing them useful, since the darkness behind his eyelids only made him think of his unfinished work. His hands were loosely open on his thighs, palms up, in the way his mother had shown him when he was small.

And the light came in.

Not the light from the window. The window-light was beautiful and cold and entirely ordinary. This was a different light, one with no direction, one that did not originate from any point in the room but seemed to originate from inside the act of perception itself. It was warm the way certain memories are warm — the way the memory of a long-dead parent, half-remembered, carries a heat that was never present in the actual moment.

It entered him through his hands first.

He felt it as pressure, then as fullness, then as something for which he had no word — a state in which the usual distances between himself and the edges of the room ceased to feel absolute. The walls were still there. The window was still there. His body was still exactly where he had left it. But there was, suddenly and unmistakably, something else in the room with him. Something that was also somehow inside the room of his chest.

He did not scream. This is, perhaps, the most remarkable thing about Gounargoun — then and later. He did not scream.

He sat very still and felt it filling him, and the word that came to him was not *invasion* and was not *gift* but was something between: *recognition*. As if this presence had been there all along, pressed against the outside of him, and only now had been allowed a way in.

*Why me?* he thought.

He would ask this question many more times before he stopped asking it. But even on that first evening, some part of him already suspected the answer: not *why* him, as though there were a criterion that had been met, but simply *him* — this specific bowl-maker, in this specific slant of autumn light, whose hands had been open and upturned for thirty years, who had prayed without wanting anything particular in return.

A god had found a door unlocked and had walked in.

## Part the Second: The Weight of Indwelling

In the days that followed, Gounargoun did not tell anyone what had happened. This was not secretiveness. It was more that he had no language for it that did not sound like boasting or madness, and he was not given to either.

The presence was constant. It did not speak in words — or if it spoke in words, they were words below the threshold of ordinary hearing, felt in the skeleton rather than perceived in the ear. He went about his work. He made bowls. He ate. He slept. And the entire time, something walked beside him inside his own chest, warm and patient and very, very large.

He was frightened. Let the record state clearly that he was frightened.

He was not frightened in the way one is frightened by a wolf at the forest edge, or by a house fire, or by the news of an enemy army in the next valley. Those were fears with clear objects, fears you could act against. This was different. He could not run from it. He could not reason with it, because it did not reason in terms he could follow. He could not pray it away, because prayer was the very channel through which it had arrived.

He began, in his fear, to resist.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. The resistance was subtle: a slight tensing when he felt the warmth begin to direct his hands toward some action he had not chosen, a mental pulling-back when he sensed a thought arise in his mind that was too luminous and too whole to have originated with him. He would think: *I did not decide that. Give it back.*

And the warmth would wait.

It was never impatient with him. It never argued. It simply waited, with a patience that made his own thirty years of aimless prayer look like the work of an afternoon. It was simply there, full of intention, full of something he could only call *purpose* — and it waited for him to stop being afraid.

This took approximately three months.

The moment of surrender, when it finally came, was not dramatic in the way the poets would later make it. Gounargoun was in his workshop. He was trying to center a piece of clay on his wheel, and it kept pulling to the left — not because of any flaw in the clay, but because of some slight irregularity in his grip that he could not correct no matter how he adjusted. He had been fighting it for the better part of an hour. He was tired. His hands hurt.

And suddenly the warmth moved into his hands. Not taking them over, not puppeting them — more like a second pair of hands covering his own, the way a teacher's hands might guide a student's, patient and sure. And the clay centered.

It was a bowl. It was, objectively, the finest bowl he had ever made. The lines were perfect. The proportion was perfect. When he lifted it from the wheel and held it in both hands, it seemed to weigh slightly less than physics permitted, as though the warmth inside him had made the clay glad.

He stood holding the bowl for a very long time.

Then he set it down and said aloud, in his workshop, to the warm presence in his chest: *All right. I am listening. What would you have me do?*

And the presence showed him.

## Part the Third: The First Great Deed — The Night of Soulless Teeth

The village of Vel Amourath lay at the edge of the Morshawl Reaches, a tract of land that had been abandoned by any god powerful enough to keep the monsters out. In those years, the soulless ones came regularly to the village border — hunched, eyeless things that had once been creatures but had eaten away every last attribute of their souls in the pursuit of some freedom they had never reached. They moved now on instinct alone. They were hungry in the only way a mindless thing can be hungry: completely.

The village had walls. The village had guards. The village had, periodically, a dead guard.

On the night that the warmth in Gounargoun's chest became fully itself for the first time, there were seven of the soulless ones at the north wall. Not wandering — gathered, which was unusual, because soulless things do not plan. Something had drawn them. Seven was more than the guards could manage.

Gounargoun knew nothing of weapons. He had a chisel and a clay knife and hands that were strong from years at the wheel, and that was the inventory of his violence before that night. He stood at the edge of his workshop, watching through the window as the torches at the north wall began to fall.

The warmth moved.

He did not feel it as a commandment. That is an error that later accounts always make — they describe divine possession as a voice saying *do this, go there, strike now*, as though a god were simply a more authoritative version of the creature's own thoughts. It was not like that. It was more like a shift in gravity. The direction of *toward* simply changed, and his body followed, because bodies follow their center of gravity.

He was walking before he had decided to walk. He was at the north wall before he had decided to go there. And standing before the first soulless one — a tall thing that had once been someone's neighbor, someone's parent, now empty of every attribute that had made it legible to other creatures — he felt the warmth become something enormous inside him.

He did not fight the monster.

He had no idea how to fight a monster. He had never trained. He had never held a sword.

What he did was more exact than fighting: the warmth in his chest extended through his hands in a way that the bowl-making had been only a small rehearsal of, and when it reached the soulless thing it did not destroy so much as it *released*. The soul fragments that had once animated this creature — shattered, scattered, clinging still to the flesh out of some dim animal habit — came loose. Not violently. The way a breath comes loose from a chest that has been holding it too long.

The creature collapsed. Not dramatically. It simply ceased to be organized.

He did this six more times before the sun came up. With each one, he felt the warmth in his chest grow slightly larger, slightly more settled, as if the act of releasing soulless things back into the nothingness they had become was a form of cleansing that worked in both directions.

Afterward he walked back to his workshop and sat down and made three bowls.

The guards who had survived the night did not know what to make of what they had seen. They agreed, without much discussion, to say nothing.

But Gounargoun knew what had happened. He had been used — used wholly and completely and in a way that left no room for him to take credit. And for the first time in his life, being used felt like being *of use*. Which was not the same thing at all, but the distinction required years to articulate.

*I am only the hand,* he told one of the guards who pressed him for an explanation.

The guard did not understand. Later, those words would be inscribed above the door of a temple.

## Part the Fourth: The Lost Truths

In the years that followed, Gounargoun walked.

He had ceased to be a bowl-maker in any exclusive sense. He still made bowls — the warmth in his chest had no interest in ending what had given him peace — but he was no longer confined to Vel Amourath in the way he had been, and when the warmth directed his feet down the road, his feet went.

He walked to the ruins of Nasbourkah, the ancient scholarly city that had been shattered in the great sectarian purges and not rebuilt. Three libraries had burned there. Scholars said nothing had survived. The warmth in his chest led him to a basement under what had once been the third library, now a cellar that some farmer used for storing grain. In the floor of that cellar, under a flagstone that required both the warmth's knowledge and Gounargoun's hands to move, he found twelve scrolls of linen wrapped in clay.

He could not read them. He did not pretend to be able to read them. He carried them to the nearest mage-hall and left them there without his name. The warmth showed him this was the correct action: the mages would read them, argue about them, misunderstand them, and eventually arrive at the truth by a path that required their own effort. A truth handed to someone who has not worked for it is a truth that will be dropped.

He found other things in other places. A record of the world's branching — how time had divided and divided again in the early ages when the great primordial wills had shaped everything, going back and forward, creating duplicates of places and creatures that they then populated with their intentions. He understood, reading this record, something he had only dimly apprehended before: that the world was layered, like the clay of a pot that has been built up in coils — each time-branch a new coil, the whole thing made coherent only by the warmth of the firing.

He brought this record to a different mage-hall. He left it there without his name.

He healed the sick in seventeen villages. Not by magic in the ordinary sense — or rather, the magic was so ordinary it was indistinguishable from extraordinary kindness. He would sit with the afflicted creature, his hands open and upturned on his knees as they had been in prayer for thirty years before the warmth arrived, and the warmth would move through him into the sickness and undo the architecture of it. He never claimed to understand this. He never claimed credit. When the families who had been healed tried to press gifts or devotion upon him, he shook his head.

*You should pray,* he told them. *Pray without wanting anything. Simply face toward the vast thing that lies behind the world and say: I am here. And then open your hands.*

Many did. Some of them, years later, were found to have become different kinds of creatures — wiser, more patient, more fully what they had been before sin and suffering had narrowed them. The warmth moved through open hands. Gounargoun knew this now with complete certainty. The warmth moved through open hands.

He navigated the puzzles of time.

This requires some explanation. In those years, there were certain dead zones in the world's temporal structure — places where a wrong branch had been created in ancient days and then abandoned, and the abandoned branch had calcified into a knot in the time-tree that prevented any creature from passing through cleanly. Monsters gathered in these zones, drawn by the distortion. Ordinary mages could not approach them without suffering severe soul damage from the temporal turbulence.

Gounargoun walked into seven of these zones and walked out of them, the knots resolved behind him. He did not understand, technically, what he was doing. The warmth understood. He was the hands.

When mages later examined the zones and found them healed, they developed elaborate theories about which laws of temporal mechanics must have been applied, and argued about the methods for generations. The correct answer — that a bowlmaker had walked in, open-handed, and that something much larger than the bowlmaker had done the actual work — was available in the record but consistently underread.

## Part the Fifth: The Awed and the Afraid

There came a time when Gounargoun was no longer a secret.

He did not want to be known. This is well attested. Every account of him from this period notes the same quality: he moved quickly, he spoke briefly, he deflected credit with a consistency that bordered on a kind of stubbornness. But the healed creatures remembered. The surviving guards remembered. The mages in the halls where he had left the scrolls could not avoid noticing that a creature had come and gone and left behind things that changed the structure of their scholarship, and they wrote this down.

Three types of creatures emerged in response to what Gounargoun had become.

The first type was the awed. These creatures looked at Gounargoun and saw what they wanted to see: a proof that the gods cared, that divine connection was real, that it was possible to be both humble and extraordinary. They brought their sick relatives to him. They wrote his name on the doors of their houses as a ward against the soulless ones. They came to him with their hands open and asked him to fill them.

He tried, patiently, to explain that he was not the source. He tried to explain the open hands, the years of empty prayer, the waiting. Most of the awed did not want this explanation. They wanted a vessel they could pour their hope into, and they had found one.

The second type was the afraid. These creatures — many of them powerful, many of them mages who had spent decades accumulating the kind of scholarly authority that depends on no one else in the room being obviously more connected to the divine than you are — looked at Gounargoun and felt the ground shift. A bowlmaker had done what they had not. A bowlmaker who could not read half the texts they had read, who had no formal training in temporal mechanics or magical theory, had healed the sick and resolved the time-knots and slain the soulless ones, all without a coherent technical explanation for any of it.

Some of these creatures grew rigid with jealousy. Some of them whispered that he was a fraud, that the warmth he described was a demon and not a god at all, that the dissolved monsters had been weakened by some other agency before he arrived. Jealousy is a precise instrument; it finds, with great accuracy, exactly the explanation that diminishes.

Gounargoun bore them no resentment. The warmth in his chest had no language for resentment. When he was in the presence of those who wished to diminish him, the warmth simply waited, with its accustomed patience, for the diminishing to run out.

The third type was the curious. These were the rarest. These were the creatures who looked at Gounargoun and asked not *can I have what he has* and not *how can I discredit what he has* but simply: *what is it like?*

He tried to answer this, when they asked.

*Imagine,* he said once, to a young scholar who had followed him for three days before working up the courage to ask, *that you have been making bowls your whole life. Your hands know the clay. You know the weight, the temperature, the resistance. You know where the clay will pull if you grip too tightly or release too soon. And then imagine that one day, without warning, a second pair of hands covers yours — hands that know not only the clay you are working but every clay that has ever been worked, every bowl that has ever been made, every fire that has ever hardened a vessel into its final form. And those hands are not taking over. They are showing. They are offering. They are waiting to feel you follow, because the bowl that results from your hands and those hands together is better than what either could make alone.*

The young scholar wrote this down. *But you,* she pressed him, *you — where are you, in that moment? When the second hands cover yours?*

Gounargoun considered this for a long time.

*I am exactly where I was,* he said finally. *Only larger.*

## Part the Sixth: The Purification of the Soul

Those who observed Gounargoun across the full span of his walking years noted a change that occurred slowly, so slowly it was only visible to those who had known him from the beginning.

He became, gradually, more still.

Not quieter — he had always been quiet. But the stillness was something that had a different quality from the stillness of a creature at rest. A creature at rest is still because it is waiting to become active again. Gounargoun's stillness was not waiting for anything. It was already arrived.

His soul was changing. The mages who studied these matters — the few who were not merely jealous, who were genuinely trying to understand — noted that a creature who regularly slays the soulless ones, who resolves temporal damage, who heals the sick, who receives the willing sacrifices of others, will have a soul that becomes, over time, almost entirely light. The attributes of the soul do not merely restore; they refine. A soul purified past a certain threshold becomes something different in kind from what it was at its original soundness.

The old texts called this *surpassing soundness*. The state beyond a hundred. The mathematically impossible score.

No one had ever reliably documented it in a living creature before Gounargoun.

What the texts said, and what the mages found themselves verifying, was that a soul purified past the threshold of its original capacity becomes, in a sense, larger than the creature it belongs to. The soul is the link between a creature and the divine. Strengthen that link past a certain point and the link begins to carry not merely a creature's signal to the divine but the divine's signal back — fully, without distortion, without the usual attenuation that comes from the imperfection of mortal flesh.

Which is to say: Gounargoun was becoming, by slow degrees, a very clear window.

He was still himself. His preferences were still his own. He still made bowls, occasionally, when he had access to clay and a wheel. He still prayed every evening with his hands open on his knees. He still answered questions with the patient precision of a man who had spent thirty years in a workshop learning to get something exactly right.

But the light that moved through him was brighter now. And the distinction between the light and the medium through which it moved had grown, if not impossible to find, then very difficult.

He did not speak of this. He had nothing to add to it by speaking. It simply was.

## Part the Seventh: The Enemy Without a Name

In the fifty-third year of Gounargoun's life — the scholars argue about the counting; some say sixty, some say less — word came to him of a thing that had been growing in the deep wilderness beyond the Vel Amourath Reaches.

The thing had no name, because naming requires someone to have survived the encounter in sufficient condition to describe it. It was not a single soulless creature. It was what happens when the soul fragments of a great many soulless creatures accumulate in one place, over many years, drawn together by the gravity of shared destruction. Each individual creature that had dissolved into monstrousness had shed its soul fragments into the world — not dead, exactly, but scattered, no longer coherent, floating in the ambient dark. And fragments, over time, collect.

What they form when they collect is something older scholars had described only in theoretical terms: a body made not of flesh but of accumulated absence. All the sins that had destroyed all those souls, pressed together, given a shape by the sheer pressure of their convergence. Not evil in the way a creature is evil — a creature who chooses evil is still a creature, still legible, still reachable. This was evil in the way that a winter storm is cold: because it could not be otherwise, because being otherwise was not available to it.

It had been consuming the Reaches for years. Villages that had been there were not there. The monsters that had prowled the borders had been absorbed into it, which made it larger and made the borders silent, which was in some ways worse than the noise.

Six companies of mages had gone to confront it. None had returned intact. The mages who survived — fewer, each time — reported the same thing: they could not fight it because their magic, which drew on the divine, found nothing to grip. The divine order works through souls. This thing had no soul. It had only the fragments of destroyed souls, which were not the same.

You cannot heal a wound that has become the patient.

When word of this reached Gounargoun, the warmth in his chest did not surge with urgency. It did not prepare for combat. It settled, quietly and completely, into a stillness that he recognized — not from his own experience, but from the thirty years of empty prayer that had come before the warmth arrived.

*Ah,* he thought. *This is what we have been coming toward.*

He walked to the Reaches.

## Part the Eighth: The Final Passage

He walked for four days. On the first day he passed through the ruins of two villages. On the second day he passed through the silence where three more had been. On the third day he passed through a landscape that had no analogue in the language of ordinary geography — the land itself had been warped by the accumulated absence of so much soul, and the trees stood at angles that made no sense in a world with consistent gravity, and the light fell wrong.

On the fourth day he found it.

It was large the way a night sky is large: not in the sense of being clearly bounded, but in the sense that it filled the available perception completely and left no room to wonder about what lay behind it. It was present in the way that silence is present after a long noise: as a kind of pressure.

He stood before it in a field where the grass had bleached to the color of old bone.

He was aware of everything that made him himself, clearly and without sentiment: his hands, which knew the clay; his evenings, which had been thirty years of open prayer; the warmth, which had entered through those open hands and had been slowly becoming what it was through all the years since; the bowls, which he still made.

He was also aware of what the thing before him was: not a creature, not an enemy in the conventional sense, but an accumulation of precisely what happens when creatures choose, again and again, the path that leads away from the divine. Every sin that had ever been committed with full knowledge and full deliberation, pressed together, given mass.

He did not reach for the warmth. He did not need to reach; it was already fully present, had been fully present since the autumn evening in the workshop when it had first arrived. He simply stood, hands open at his sides, and the warmth became everything he was capable of being.

And then he did not fight.

This is the part of the account that the later traditions argue about most fiercely. The fighting tradition says he unleashed the god's power against the thing and obliterated it. The mystic tradition says he dissolved into the divine completely at the moment of contact and carried the thing with him into annihilation. Both traditions are reading the same account and finding the battle they expected.

What the account says is simpler and stranger than either.

He stood before the accumulated absence of a thousand shattered souls, and he forgave it.

This is not to say he forgave the sins that had created it — the murders and the deceits and the deliberate splittings of time and the willful morphings of things that should have been left themselves. Those had their own accounting, elsewhere, in the divine order's deep arithmetic. He forgave the thing that had resulted. He looked at what despair, taken to its logical conclusion across a thousand separate lives, had built — this structure of consequence and darkness, this body made of everything that had been abandoned — and he recognized it for what it was: a door that had been locked from both sides.

He opened his hands.

The warmth moved through him and it moved through the accumulated absence and it reached the soul fragments that had been turning in that dark place for years — the last remnants of what those creatures had been, before the choosing, before the sinning — and it released them. Not destroying them. Releasing them from the obligation of holding together a shape that their destruction had forced upon them.

The fragments came apart.

Not violently. The way a breath comes loose from a chest that has been holding it too long.

The thing dissolved. Not like a fire going out — more like an argument resolving, the kind where neither side was entirely wrong, and the resolution is simply that the terms of the disagreement have been superseded.

The bleached field was quiet.

Gounargoun stood in it for a moment that was, in the account's telling, outside the usual structure of moments.

And then he was not there.

## Part the Ninth: What Remains

The scholars argue about what happened to Gounargoun. The arguments are of two broad types, and they have continued, in various forms, for as long as the text has been read.

The first type says he died — that the act of fully channeling the divine through mortal flesh was, at its limit, consumptive, and that what was left of the creature at the moment of completion was simply not enough to remain alive. This argument has a certain mechanical respectability. It explains the disappearance. It maintains the neat separation between creature and god.

The second type says he became something else entirely — that the process the text describes, in which a soul purified past its own capacity becomes something different in kind, had reached its conclusion, and what remained was neither creature nor god but a third category for which the world's ontology had not previously had room. Neither dying nor surviving, but *arriving* at a state that the text calls nirvana and that the scholars have argued about the definition of ever since.

The unnamed transcriber of this foreword will not adjudicate this argument. But the unnamed transcriber will note the following, which seems worth recording:

The soul is the link between a creature and the divine. It is the channel through which gods perceive and touch the world, and through which creatures — in the other direction — reach toward something larger than themselves. The god perceives through time. The soul operates within time. The link is a temporal structure.

What the text describes Gounargoun as becoming — this third state, this nirvana — is explicitly described as being beyond the reach of both gods and creatures. Not beyond their power. Beyond their *perception*. Beyond time, which is the medium through which all perception in this world is organized.

A being outside of time entirely cannot be found by a god. Cannot be found by a creature. Cannot be found, perhaps, even by itself in the way that things are found and categorized and returned to.

The unnamed transcriber does not know if this is a good thing or a terrible one. The text does not seem to think it is either. The text describes it with the same tone it uses for the opening autumn light in the workshop, which is to say: with the reverence appropriate to something that simply is, and is not in need of evaluation.

Gounargoun spent thirty years with open hands before the warmth arrived. He spent the years of his walking life as the hand through which the warmth moved. And then the hand and the warmth arrived, together, at a door the text calls final — and walked through it into a state where the distinction between hand and warmth no longer resolved into two things.

What remains?

The bowls remain. There are, in several temple collections, a number of fired clay bowls attributed to Gounargoun's hand — simple, perfect, exactly proportioned, slightly lighter than physics would seem to permit. They are unremarkable to look at. They hold water.

The account remains, which is this.

And in the village of Vel Amourath — smaller now, the mages say, but still there — the people still pray in the evenings with their hands open on their knees, turned toward the vast thing that lies behind the world, saying: *I know you are there. I do not understand you. I am here.*

Not waiting for an answer.

Already giving one.
